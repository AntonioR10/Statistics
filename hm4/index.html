Law of Large Numbers

E[Sn/n] = μ and Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n → 0. By Chebyshev, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0, so Sn/n converges in probability to μ (p for Bernoulli, 0 for Rademacher). The table compares the simulated tail probability with this bound, which is valid but loose.

Variance under the three normalizations

Var(Sn) = nσ² grows, Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n shrinks, and only Var(Sn/√n) = σ² stays constant. This is why √n is the right scale for the fluctuations of Sn around its mean: dividing by n over-shrinks them, and not dividing at all lets them blow up.

Central Limit Theorem

For the Rademacher model μ = 0 and σ = 1, so Sn/√n converges in distribution to N(0, 1). The last plot uses the standardized score Zn = (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n), which equals Sn/√n in the Rademacher case and also converges to N(0, 1) for Bernoulli. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance in the table decreases with n until it reaches the sampling noise set by m. For Bernoulli, Sn/√n alone has mean p√n and drifts away: the CLT applies only after centring.

Reading the model as n systems and m attackers

  • Bernoulli: Sn is the number of systems an attacker breaks. Its mean np grows linearly, but the fraction Sn/n becomes predictable as n grows. Large installations have a stable compromise rate, while the absolute count fluctuates by about √(np(1−p)).
  • Rademacher: each step is a gain or a setback, with no drift. An attacker wanders around 0 with typical excursions of order √n, which is negligible compared with n.
  • System size n: increases the absolute spread of Sn (like √n) but decreases the relative spread (like 1/√n).
  • Number of attackers m: does not change the distribution of one attacker's score. It improves our estimate of that distribution: histograms get smoother and the KS noise floor falls roughly like 1/√m. The total over all attackers has mean mnp and variance mnσ².
Limits of the model: real systems are correlated, attackers adapt, and p differs between systems. Independence and identical distribution are what make these theorems apply.
