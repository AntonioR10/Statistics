Homework: Measure Theory & Numerical CDF Approximation

Ex. 1 – Riemann vs Lebesgue Integration

1. Conceptual Overview

  • Riemann (midpoint sum): partition $[a,b]$ into $N$ subintervals, $R_N=\sum f(x_i^*)\Delta x$. Cost $O(N)$ evaluations of $f$.
  • Lebesgue (range partition): partition the range $[y_{\min},y_{\max}]$ into $N$ bins and sum $L_N=\sum_j \tilde y_j\,\mu(\{y_j\le fdistribution function $G(y)=\mu(\{f\ge y\})$, computed analytically (or by bisection for the monotone $\sin x/x$), so no sampling of the domain is involved. The bin measure is $G(y_j)-G(y_{j+1})$ (the last bin is closed on the right). Cost $O(N)$ evaluations of $G$.
  • For $1/\sqrt{x}$ (unbounded) the range is truncated at $y_{\max}=\sqrt N$, which balances truncation error $\approx 1/y_{\max}$ and discretization error $\approx y_{\max}/N$.

2. Pathological Cases: Dirichlet & Thomae

  • Dirichlet $D=\mathbb I_{\mathbb Q}$ on $[0,1]$: every subinterval contains rationals and irrationals, so upper Darboux sum $U=1$, lower $L=0$ for every partition: not Riemann-integrable. Since $\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]$ is countable, $\mu(\mathbb Q)=0$ and $\int D\,d\mu=1\cdot 0+0\cdot 1=0$.
  • Thomae $T(p/q)=1/q$, $T=0$ on irrationals: continuous at every irrational and discontinuous exactly on $\mathbb Q$ (a null set), hence Riemann-integrable (Lebesgue criterion) with $\int T=0$. The upper sums $U_N\to 0$, while $L_N=0$. Lebesgue gives $0$ because $T=0$ almost everywhere.

Dirichlet and Thomae cannot be sampled on a computer (floating-point numbers are all rational), so they are treated through Darboux sums and exact measure arguments, not through a numerical evaluation of $f$.

4. Discussion

  • Same value? For Riemann-integrable functions both sums converge to the same integral (see the errors in the table). For $1/\sqrt x$ the Riemann integral exists only as an improper integral, while the Lebesgue integral exists directly ($f\in L^1$); both approach $2$ but slowly, with error $\sim N^{-1/2}$.
  • Accuracy/convergence: the measured order is printed under the table (log-ratio of the last two errors). Smooth functions: midpoint Riemann is $O(N^{-2})$; Lebesgue with midpoint values is $O(N^{-1})$ in general, and exact or near-exact for piecewise-constant functions (step, indicator), where only a few bins carry mass.
  • Efficiency: both are $O(N)$, but Lebesgue needs the preimage measure in closed form; where it is not available (generic $f$) it must be estimated by sampling the domain, which is itself a Riemann-type sum and costs $O(N\cdot M)$. The Lebesgue viewpoint pays off theoretically (Dominated Convergence, $L^1$ completeness, Dirichlet), not as a faster numerical scheme.

Ex. 2 – Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

1. Why $\Phi$ is not elementary (Liouville)

$$\Phi(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-t^2/2}\,dt$$

Liouville's theorem (Hogg–McKean–Craig, §3.4 notes): let $f,g$ be rational functions with $g$ non-constant. Then $\int f e^{g}\,dt$ is elementary if and only if there is a rational $R$ with $R'+g'R=f$, in which case the integral is $R\,e^{g}$.

Here $f=1$, $g=-t^2/2$, so we would need $R'-tR=1$. If $R$ had a pole of order $m$ at a finite point, $R'$ would have order $m+1$ and $tR$ order $m$, so they cannot cancel. Hence $R$ is a polynomial of degree $n$ and $R'-tR$ has degree $n+1\ge1$ (leading coefficient $-a_n\neq0$), which cannot equal the constant $1$ (and $R=0$ gives $0\ne1$). No rational $R$ exists, so $e^{-t^2/2}$ has no elementary antiderivative.

2. Error function: exact in form, numerical in practice

With $\operatorname{erf}(z)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^z e^{-u^2}du$ and $u=t/\sqrt2$: $$\Phi(x)=\tfrac12\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt2}\right)\right]$$ This is an exact analytic representation, but erf is just a name for a non-elementary function: to get a number one still needs a series, a continued fraction, a rational approximation or quadrature. Library implementations (e.g. Cody's rational minimax) are exactly such approximations.

3. Methods implemented

  • Trapezoid / Simpson on $[-10,x]$ ($-\infty$ replaced by $-10$, neglected tail $<10^{-23}$), $N=2000$.
  • Taylor erf, 20 terms: $\operatorname{erf}(z)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\sum_k\frac{(-1)^k z^{2k+1}}{k!(2k+1)}$. Fails for $|x|\gtrsim3$ (truncation).
  • Taylor erf, adaptive: stops when terms are below $10^{-17}$ of the sum. Converges, but the alternating series suffers cancellation (terms up to $e^{z^2}$) and the final $\tfrac12(1+\operatorname{erf})$ loses relative accuracy in the left tail.
  • Abramowitz–Stegun 7.1.26: rational-times-exponential approximation, $|\varepsilon|\le1.5\cdot10^{-7}$ (it is itself an approximation).
  • Hybrid: for $|x|<3$ the cancellation-free series $\Phi(x)=\tfrac12+\varphi(x)\sum_k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}$ (all terms same sign); for $|x|\ge3$ the Laplace continued fraction for the tail $Q(x)=\varphi(x)/\bigl(x+\tfrac{1}{x+\tfrac{2}{x+\tfrac{3}{x+\cdots}}}\bigr)$, which keeps full relative accuracy.
