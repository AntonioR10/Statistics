Ex. 1 – Riemann vs Lebesgue Integration

1. Conceptual Overview

The fundamental distinction between Riemann and Lebesgue integration lies in how the domain/range is partitioned:

  • Riemann Integration: Partitions the domain $[a, b]$ into subintervals of length $\Delta x_i$. It computes sums of the form $\sum f(x_i^*) \Delta x_i$. It struggles with highly discontinuous functions.
  • Lebesgue Integration: Partitions the range $[y_{\min}, y_{\max}]$ into subintervals of length $\Delta y_j$. It computes sums of the form $\sum y_j \cdot \mu(f^{-1}([y_j, y_{j+1}))$, where $\mu$ is the Lebesgue measure of the preimage.

2. Pathological Cases: Dirichlet & Thomae

Consider the Dirichlet function $D(x) = \mathbb{I}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)$ on $[0, 1]$:

  • Riemann: For any partition, every subinterval contains both rationals and irrationals. Thus, the upper Darboux sum is $1$ and the lower sum is $0$. The Riemann integral fails to exist.
  • Lebesgue: Since $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$ is countable, its measure is $\mu(\mathbb{Q}) = 0$. The preimage of $1$ has measure $0$, and the preimage of $0$ has measure $1$. Thus, $\int_{[0, 1]} D \, d\mu = 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 = 0$.

Ex. 2 – Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

1. Non-Elementarity & Liouville's Theorem

The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of the Standard Normal Distribution is defined as: $$\Phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-\frac{t^2}{2}} \, dt$$

By Liouville's Theorem (and Differential Algebra), the integrand $e^{-t^2/2}$ does not have an antiderivative expressible in terms of elementary functions (polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, or trigonometric functions). Thus, $\Phi(x)$ must be evaluated using numerical techniques or special functions.

2. Relationship to the Error Function (erf)

The standard error function is defined as $\text{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^x e^{-u^2} du$. Through a change of variables $u = t/\sqrt{2}$, we obtain the analytical equivalence: $$\Phi(x) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ 1 + \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \right]$$

3. Series Expansion Implementation

Using Taylor expansion for $e^{-u^2} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k u^{2k}}{k!}$, we derive the power series for $\text{erf}(z)$: $$\text{erf}(z) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k z^{2k+1}}{k! (2k + 1)}$$
