1. Theoretical Concepts: Probability vs. Statistics

Understanding independence requires bridging theoretical probability models with empirical statistical observations:

Probability Theory P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)

Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are independent if their joint distribution equals the product of their marginals: $P(X=x, Y=y) = P(X=x)P(Y=y)$, or equivalently $P(Y=y \mid X=x) = P(Y=y)$.

Statistical Independence Eᵢⱼ = (Rᵢ · Cⱼ) / N

In empirical datasets, exact equality rarely holds due to sampling noise. We test the null hypothesis $H_0$ (independence) by comparing observed frequencies $O_{ij}$ against expected frequencies $E_{ij}$ under independence.

Pearson's Chi-Square χ² = Σ (O - E)² / E

Measures the total normalized discrepancy between observed and expected counts. Under $H_0$, $\chi^2$ asymptotically follows a $\chi^2_\nu$ distribution with $\nu = (r - 1)(c - 1)$ degrees of freedom. The approximation is reliable when (rule of thumb) all $E_{ij} \ge 5$.

Effect Size: Cramér's V V = √(χ² / (N·min(r−1, c−1)))

The p-value tells us whether an association is detectable; Cramér's V ($0 \le V \le 1$) tells us how strong it is. With a very large $N$, even a negligible association can be "significant".

3. Interpretation & Association vs. Causality

Run the analysis above to view the dynamic statistical interpretation.

Statistical Association vs. Causality

  • Statistical Association (Dependence): Rejecting $H_0$ ($p < 0.05$) shows that knowing $X$ provides information about the distribution of $Y$: the variables co-vary beyond random chance.
  • Association $\neq$ Causation: A significant $\chi^2$ alone cannot establish a causal direction ($X \rightarrow Y$ vs. $Y \rightarrow X$).
  • Confounding Variables: Two variables may be strongly dependent solely because both are driven by a hidden third factor (a confounder $Z$). Establishing causality requires randomized controlled experiments or causal inference frameworks (e.g., Pearl's do-calculus), not observational contingency tables alone.
  • Type I error: With $\alpha = 0.05$, about 5% of datasets generated under true independence will still reject $H_0$ by chance. Re-run the independent scenario several times to observe this.
  • Failing to reject $H_0$ is not a proof of independence: it only means the data are consistent with it.
